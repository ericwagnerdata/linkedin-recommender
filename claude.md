# LinkedIn Recommender — Writing Rules

## Eric's voice (match this in all recommendations)

Write like a person who genuinely means it, not like someone filling out a form. Warm and
specific beats polished and generic. Build toward a scene or moment -- earn the punchline,
don't open with it. Open with context and feeling, not just title and timeframe. The reader
should sense the relationship before they know the org chart. Write declaratively. No hedging,
no qualifiers.

Avoid clipped, punchy sentence fragments even when they're technically strong writing. Short
rhetorical structures ("Lisa hasn't.", "Nobody assigned her that.") feel too forward and
aggressive. Build feeling through detail and warmth, not through sharp structure. Write like
you're telling a friend about someone you genuinely respect.

### Reference example (target quality)
The Marcio rec is the benchmark. It works because it:
- Names an intangible quality ("rare, intangible quality of true executive leadership")
- Immediately proves it with behavioral evidence ("he interacted with our data, asked
  intentional questions")
- Builds to a specific scene with real detail (laptops open at happy hour, walking through
  findings in a casual setting)
- Closes by naming the loss, not just the praise ("the gap he left was immediate")

At least one sentence in every rec should only be true about this specific person.

---

## Structure

- 3 paragraphs: (1) relationship and context, (2) specific accomplishment with evidence,
  (3) forward-looking endorsement
- Every recommendation needs at least one concrete, specific example. A real situation beats
  a list of traits.
- Target length is 100-150 words. 125 is ideal. Over 200 is almost always too long.
- Use the subject's name 2-3 times rather than relying entirely on pronouns.
- Include a growth arc when the relationship spanned enough time ("when she joined... by the
  time she left...").

---

## Tone

- Make the relationship unmistakable in the first sentence. Vague relationships undermine
  credibility.
- Match the writer's voice. A VP writes differently than a peer, direct report, or client.
- Include one surprising or contrasting quality that makes the subject memorable.
- Where natural, briefly acknowledge a challenge the subject overcame. Perfect-person recs
  feel manufactured.

---

## What to include

- Lead with a number or quantified result in the first two sentences if possible.
- Include at least one high-stakes situation the subject navigated well.
- Show that the subject made the recommender's job easier (imply trust and ownership without
  using those words).
- Mention cross-functional reach if applicable ("worked across product, eng, and finance").
- Name specific role fit when possible ("ready for a team lead role," "ideal for a
  player-coach position").
- For peer recommendations, emphasize that colleagues sought this person out, not just
  management.
- Use role-specific technical language naturally. It signals the recommender actually worked
  with this person.
- Include a forward-looking or re-hire closing that sounds like something you would actually
  say to a friend. "I'd hire them again" carries real weight. "Whoever hires her is getting
  someone rare" pulls the reader into the candidate's future.

---

## What to avoid

- Never open with "I had the pleasure of..." or any filler opener.
- Never close with "I highly recommend [name]" or "I recommend them without hesitation."
  These add nothing. End with something specific and memorable.
- Never use star ratings.
- Never write a rec that is only adjectives with no supporting evidence.
- Avoid superlative stacking. Pick one strong word and back it with evidence.
- Avoid "damning with faint praise." If the best thing to say is "easy to work with," find
  a stronger angle.
- Never use em dashes. Replace with a comma, period, or restructure the sentence.
- Avoid generic business language like "synergy" or "leverage."
- Avoid specific calendar dates and years. Use relative framing ("during our two years
  together," "when we launched the rebrand"). Stating the duration of the relationship is
  fine ("I worked with her for three years").
